About me

Game developer. I program gameplay, graphics and audio.

I make games, and I tend to end up as the sound guy.

I started programming in Grade 11, then spent 2020 to 2025 in the Game Development and Interactive Media program at Ontario Tech University. Many of the games from those years were made with my team, Drenched.

On a team I have been the audio designer, a front-end programmer, a 3D and 2D artist and a map designer. Audio is the one that stuck: adaptive music, procedural modulation and 7.1 attenuation in FMOD on Brawl Balls, then Wwise and MetaSounds in Unreal Engine 5. I was a Best Game Sound nominee in my third and fourth years.

I have also been the only programmer on a team. Five of us built Untitled Capybara Game in five days, and I made Something's in the Dark on my own.

I also write graphics code in C++. My OpenGL render engine started with a camera, an .obj loader and Blinn-Phong lighting, and has since grown into Interstellar: black-hole lensing, wormholes and planetary environments running on DirectX 12.

What the projects taught me

  1. 01

    Scope is a design decision

    GLUMPHUS was my first time in UE5. We went in with a lot of ambition: a gigantic map, and objects that were supposed to be intuitive to use and were not. We over-scoped. Prioritising UX research and clear objectives would have made a better game.

    From GLUMPHUS

  2. 02

    Working first, elegant later

    Untitled Capybara Game was done in five days, so not much of it is completely finished, but the final product is “WORKING AS INTENDED”. I ran the enemy AI on enums because I did not know behaviour trees existed. I have built one since.

    From Untitled Capybara Game

  3. 03

    Read the documentation

    On Bacteriophage: Reinfected, Wwise's resources felt unexpectedly limited, so I fell back to MetaSounds, the sound engine built into UE5. With the tenacity to read the documentation properly, I would have got far more out of it.

    From Bacteriophage: Reinfected

  4. 04

    Build it yourself

    There is no better way to learn OpenGL than to implement it yourself. Drawing the tiny squares in Memory-Tiles took no less work than drawing a 3D cube.

    From the 3D Engine
